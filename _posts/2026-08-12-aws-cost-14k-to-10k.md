The largest AWS account I manage was billing roughly $14k a month. Over a few months of deliberate work it came down to about $10k, and it has stayed there. Nothing about how the workloads behave changed from a user's point of view.

The uncomfortable summary: almost none of the savings came from clever engineering. They came from finding things nobody had turned off. Cloud cost work is mostly an inventory problem wearing an optimization costume.

## Where the money actually was

When people imagine cost optimization they imagine rightsizing exercises, Savings Plans spreadsheets, and Graviton migrations. Those are real, but they are the second act. The first act — the one with most of the money in it — was answering a simpler question: *what is running, and does anyone still want it?*

The recurring categories, in roughly descending order of impact:

- **Non-production compute running 24/7.** A development instance that is only used during working hours runs about 3.4x longer than it needs to. Nobody decided this; it is just the default, because turning things off requires someone to own the act of turning them off.
- **Environments that outlived their project.** Staging stacks for features that shipped a year ago. A load test environment from a launch that already happened. Each one small, each one invisible, none with an owner.
- **Orphaned resources.** Unattached EBS volumes, aged snapshots with no source volume, idle load balancers still passing health checks to nothing, NAT gateways serving subnets with no workloads. These bill precisely because they do nothing — nothing errors, so nothing gets attention.
- **Oversized by inheritance.** Instances sized during a panic two years ago and never revisited, because "it works, don't touch it."

## What made it stick

One-off cleanups decay. Six months later the account is back where it was, because the *behavior* that created the waste is untouched. Three mechanisms made this one permanent:

**Forecast alerts, not just actual-spend alerts.** An actual-spend alert tells you money is already gone. AWS Budgets' forecast alert fires when the month is *trending* over, which is the only version that gives you time to act inside the same billing cycle.

**Scheduled shutdown, selected by tag.** A scheduled Lambda stops tagged non-production instances in the evening and starts them in the morning. Two design decisions mattered more than the code. First, selection is by tag, so onboarding an instance is a tagging decision, not a code change — the barrier to participation is nearly zero. Second, the IAM policy carries an `aws:ResourceTag` condition, so the function is *physically unable* to stop an untagged instance. The blast radius is bounded by the platform, not by my code being correct.

**Waste detection as a standing alarm, not an audit.** The one-off audit that found the orphaned resources became a scheduled scan that pages when it finds new ones. The point is to catch the drift while the person who created the resource still remembers why.

The reusable parts of this programme are published as [terraform-aws-cost-guardrails](https://github.com/arunrajm91/terraform-aws-cost-guardrails).

## What I would tell someone starting this

Don't open the rightsizing spreadsheet first. Open Cost Explorer grouped by service, then by tag (and note how much spend is *untagged* — that number is your real problem). Everything with no owner is a candidate for being turned off, and the honest way to find out is to schedule the shutdown and see who complains. In my experience, for genuinely abandoned resources: nobody does.

The last thousand dollars was ten times harder than the first three. That is the correct place to stop. A cost programme that starts dictating architecture to save the final 5% has inverted its own purpose.
